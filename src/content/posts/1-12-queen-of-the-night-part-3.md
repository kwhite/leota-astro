## When last we met…
The crew met the infamous Queen of the Night, Yasmin Farhat, and agreed to provide their help dealing with her adversaries in exchange for shelter in the Undertrash. They spent the night in tight quarters that led to uncomfortable conversations all around.

## Pulling at Threads
> Maeve: “Creative ideation is not something you **fix**, Daniel.”<br>
> Geoffrey: “Oh my God. Oh my God, oh my God. He's just got to crash and burn on his own. I can't save this thing. There's nothing I can do. He's gonna have to learn.”

Over breakfast, the crew revisits what they think they know. Daniel gives them the full (pompous) download of what he realized during his study of the crime board in Maeve’s apartment. The first revelation: the Men in Gray are not associated with Armand Short’s operation. Both do have ties to the Niners, the police force in Independence. Cillian shares that they’re easily the most corrupt district in the City, and it’s likely that Armand has a standing “arrangement” with them to send any fertility deities that enter the Town Hall facility his way. That would explain why Maya was taken to the Barn instead of handed over to the Men in Gray. Duke’s not the only cop on his payroll.

Then there's Tío Tez, whose absence is already destabilizing La Colonia de Sombras. Armand has three of the four Tezcatlipocas, and the fourth is Tez's daughter, Erica. Her whereabouts are unknown. This ties to Daniel’s second realization from studying the board: Tez likely had a playbook in the event something happened to him. So he would have arranged for her to be abducted and hidden to keep her out of enemy hands. That’s why Armand can’t find her.

The four Tezcatlipocas are creator gods, but they are also destroyers. Together, they possess the power to make and unmake worlds. What would Armand Short do with that kind of power?

### Flashback
London stands on the riverbank studying Armand Short during their brief, tense conversation moments before the crew makes their escape into the water. He searches for an angle to play with Armand, peering through the mist to see to the heart of the man — what motivates him, what he wants above all things. 

What he discovers is deceptively simple: **gold, God, and power.**

---
Kevin relays that Armand has a Rift, and it is Cortés. That changes the conversation considerably, and helps London put what he learned about Armand’s motivations into context. Cortés did not conquer the Aztec Empire through Spanish military strength alone. He exploited existing divisions, building alliances with Indigenous peoples who had their own reasons to oppose the Triple Alliance. The conquest depended in no small part on persuading others to help dismantle the very political structures that had governed them. 

> London: "He used our hubris against us. He was a brilliant tactician."

(There is a brief interruption to establish whether Cortés was also responsible for the cursed pirate gold in *Pirates of the Caribbean*.)

> Geoffrey: “In the video game world, we call those 4X games.”

But Cortés was more than a conqueror seeking wealth. His religious convictions were inseparable from his ambitions, and the gods worshipped by the people he conquered were false idols to be eradicated. He might want more wealth and influence, but it’s unlikely that either would really satisfy him. He wanted the systematic, intentional dismantling of an entire culture.

And then the pieces begin to fit together.

> London: “Armand is only acting according to type, I suppose.”

Armand is gathering the four Tezcatlipocas, gods capable of creating and destroying worlds. He has already demonstrated that he can compel supernatural beings to obey him. Cortés once turned the people and political structures of the Aztec empire against it. What if Armand intends to do the same thing again, only this time with its gods instead?

London proposes that Armand means to use the four Tezcatlipocas in a ritual to destroy the other deities that have manifested in the City, eliminating both the false idols that offend his religious convictions and the supernatural powers that could stand between him and his ambitions.

It's a theory rather than a confirmed explanation of the ritual, but it fits what the crew has discovered. And Armand already has three of the four beings he needs to pull it off.

## Two Roads
The implications are alarming enough that the crew decides to divide its efforts. Geoffrey, London, Wren, John, and Kevin will travel toward Mictlān to learn more about the Tezcatlipocas and the ritual Armand may be preparing. Sergei, Daniel, Maeve, and Cillian will investigate ways to protect the crew's own divine Rifts from whatever Armand intends.

The threat is no longer limited to the gods Armand has captured. If London's theory is correct, every divine Rift in the City could be at risk.

Fiona agrees to guide the Mictlān expedition through the Margins. The underground community is somewhat busier than it was during their previous visit, and its residents regard the unfamiliar travellers with understandable suspicion. As they walk, London asks Fiona about Jakub and Marcus, the two men Yasmin mentioned when discussing the competing factions beneath the City.

Fiona has little good to say about either of them. Jakub is a scarred, heavily armed brute with a badly damaged nose and an apparent enthusiasm for violence. She suspects there's an actual monster inside him, although she admits that's only a guess. Marcus Romsia, on the other hand, is ambitious, methodical, and apparently quite charming.

London recognizes Romsia’s name as a former member of Tez's organization, one of the relatively few white men who managed to earn a place in the cartel before a falling-out with its leader. Romsia now commands a faction of his own. Fiona would rather deal with Romsia than Jakub. Romsia wants power, which at least makes his behavior somewhat predictable. Jakub, she believes, wants nothing more than chaos and bloodshed.

## Family Matters
London eventually asks Fiona about Cillian Tennant. She clearly knows him, and just as clearly has reservations about discussing his affairs with strangers. Whatever has happened between them, Fiona doesn't want to see Cillian hurt, and she suggests that the wrong information reaching the wrong person could be dangerous for him and anyone close to him.

London's concern is considerably more specific. "Don't take this the wrong way, but I am much more concerned about Maeve than I am about Cillian."

Fiona doesn’t break stride as she replies, "Maybe you should be."

Fiona explains that Cillian appears to genuinely like Maeve, which is likely to cause problems with certain people. When London asks whether Fiona herself objects, she says no. Cillian's mother, however, is another matter entirely.

London immediately begins trying to determine which parts of the City Maeve should avoid. He suspects Cillian's mother has some sort of criminal ties, although Fiona neither confirms that theory nor identifies where the danger might lie. London isn't asking for the woman's secrets, at least not directly. He just wants to know where the boundaries are, because Maeve is entirely capable of investigating something dangerous simply because someone has suggested she shouldn't.

Fiona is reluctant to say more. London recognizes the calculation in her responses and tries to make it clear that he understands the value of discretion, but she has no intention of jeopardizing her own position for his benefit.

As they approach the end of the inhabited passages, Fiona finally offers one more warning: women Cillian cares about have a tendency to meet unfortunate ends. She doesn't suggest he's responsible. Instead, she implies that if Cillian begins pushing Maeve away, there may be a very good reason for it.

London has acquired some useful information, none of which is remotely reassuring.

## The Way to Mictlān
Fiona stops where the inhabited passages begin to thin out and explains the routes ahead. The most direct path to Mictlan ends at a cave-in. Another route leads back through the chamber containing the enormous serpent, then toward additional entrances, though those appear blocked as well.

The crew suspects the repeated collapses are deliberate. Someone, or something, seems determined to prevent access to the underworld, although Fiona points out that Maria apparently has a way of getting in and out.

London wonders whether the barriers are intended to exclude everyone or simply those who haven't been invited. The distinction could matter, particularly if the entrances are protected by something more sophisticated than a pile of rocks.

They choose the direct route, continuing through abandoned subway tunnels until something green and scaly emerges from a crack in the passage.

> John: “I’ve fought in a lot of bar fights, but never with a green, scaly monster. At least... that I remember."

Wren grabs a length of pipe and strikes the creature in the back of the head, while Kevin brings a chunk of loose concrete down onto its shoulder. Geoffrey, apparently recognizing an artistic opportunity, begins sketching the monster rather than helping to kill it. He suggests the image might be useful for future tattoos.

London moves in with his switchblade. It sinks into the creature, but the wound produces black blood that seeps out with unnatural slowness.

John throws a rock, and the others continue attacking until another blow from Wren’s pipe takes the creature's head off. Whatever it was, it had nothing useful on its body, so the crew left it behind and continued toward Mictlān.

Eventually, the tunnel ends in the collapse Fiona warned them about. John examines the rubble, looking for signs of what brought it down, while London searches for evidence of a more deliberate barrier. If someone has sealed the entrance, perhaps there is some way to obtain permission to pass. Nothing obvious presents itself.

With no invitation into the underworld forthcoming, John, Kevin, and London begin pulling stones from the cave-in, attempting to clear a passage through the rubble.
